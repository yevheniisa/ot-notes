# CISA VLP 100W — ICS Cybersecurity Practices

Completed 2026-11-10.

## OPSEC process (5 steps)
1. **Identify critical information** — research, contracts, budget, workforce. Ask: what is sensitive, who wants it and why, what's already public about us (research yourself), how could it cause harm.
2. **Analyze the threat** — natural (flood, earthquake), unintentional (power outage after a car hits a pole), intentional (attackers).
3. **Analyze the vulnerability** — social media, phone, web, people. People are the main risk.
4. **Assess the risk** — Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact. In OT, impact includes safety and environment, not just money.
5. **Apply countermeasures** — security tools, change procedures, training.

**OT angle:** critical information at a plant = network diagrams, IP plans, PLC/DCS models and firmware, vendor names, remote access methods, turnaround schedules. A site photo on LinkedIn with an HMI screen in the background is an OPSEC failure.

## Phishing
- **Basic** — mass emails to steal credentials.
- **Spear phishing** — targeted at a specific person or team, using details about them. Ukraine 2015 grid attack started this way.
- **Whaling** — targeted at executives.
- Defenses: employee awareness, MFA, data protection policy, social media education.

## Layers of defense
1. **MFA** — something you know (password), have (key), are (biometrics).
2. **Internet vs intranet** — intranet is the internal network. It is *not* automatically cut off from the internet; it's usually connected through firewalls. In OT the real control is segmentation (Purdue model). True air gaps are rare: vendor laptops, USB sticks and remote access cross them.
3. **Separation of duties** — no single person holds too much access.
4. **Least privilege** — access matches your tasks (same idea as file permissions in Bandit).
5. **Physical security** — guards, cameras.

## Scenario check (2026-11-10): contractor wants TeamViewer on an HMI for a week
- Not my call: escalate through my supervisor and the operator to whoever owns the control system (controls / OT lead). Not to the contractor's own company.
- Why it's bad: an always-on inbound path that bypasses the firewall and segmentation; no MFA by default; the site doesn't see or log the session; "tuning settings" remotely = changing the process without the operator and without MOC; the vendor's laptop becomes part of our attack surface.
- A "no" without an alternative gets bypassed. Offer instead: the site's approved secure remote access (jump host in the DMZ, MFA, time-limited, session recorded, operator watches and approves); read-only trends from the historian; setpoint changes through MOC; or the vendor comes on site.
